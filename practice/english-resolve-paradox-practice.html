QUESTION 1 OF 20 Comparison Paradox

The Twin Cities Paradox

  • City A and City B have nearly identical median income, population density, and fast-food restaurants per capita.
  • Higher fast-food density is well linked to higher obesity rates in national studies.
  • City A's obesity rate is markedly lower than City B's, despite the matching fast-food exposure.
WHAT WE'RE RECONCILING Similar fast-food exposure should produce similar obesity rates — yet City A's rate is markedly lower.
Which choice most helps explain this discrepancy?
Tip: In a comparison paradox, look for a variable that wasn't actually matched between the two groups.

QUESTION 3 OF 20 Trend-Break Paradox

The Housing Price Paradox

  • A city built 5,000 new housing units over three years, sharply increasing housing supply.
  • A large increase in supply is expected to push prices down or keep them flat.
  • Average home prices in the city rose by 20% over the same three years.
WHAT WE'RE RECONCILING A large supply increase should have kept prices flat or falling — yet prices rose 20%.

Tip: When supply says one thing, check whether demand moved even more in the other direction.

QUESTION 5 OF 20 Reporting Paradox

The Whistleblower Paradox

  • In the year after the hotline launched, confirmed fraud cases at the company dropped to nearly zero.
WHAT WE'RE RECONCILING A new hotline should increase fraud detection — yet confirmed fraud cases nearly vanished.

WHAT WE'RE RECONCILING Easier recycling should have diverted material away from the landfill — yet landfill waste stayed the same.

Tip: A rising 'input' number doesn't guarantee the intended outcome — check what happens to the material after collection.
